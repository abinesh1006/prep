Two Heaps

Split the numbers into a small half and a big half, each in its own heap; the median is always sitting on top of one or both of them.

Time to learn: about 1–2 hours · Prerequisites: Heaps, Top K

When to use it — the signal

"Median of a data stream", "running median", "middle value as numbers arrive", "sliding window median", "balance two groups so one side is always ≤ the other".

The idea in plain words

Numbers arrive one at a time: 5, 15, 1, 3, 8, 7. After each one, you must say the median (the middle value of the sorted list; with an even count, the average of the two middle values).

Re-sorting after every number costs O(n log n) each time. Instead, keep the numbers in two groups:

  • low: the smaller half, in a max-heap — its top is the biggest of the small numbers.
  • high: the bigger half, in a min-heap — its top is the smallest of the big numbers.

Keep two promises: every number in low ≤ every number in high, and the sizes differ by at most one (low may hold one extra). Then the middle of the sorted list is right at the tops: odd count → low.peek(); even count → the average of both tops.

Adding a number is a couple of heap operations, O(log n). Reading the median is O(1).

Analogy

A class lines up by height and splits into two rooms of equal size: short kids in one room, tall kids in the other. The tallest short kid and the shortest tall kid stand at the doors. The "middle height" is always one of the two kids at the doors — you never need to look inside the rooms.

Same idea, written as "place, then rebalance"

Many people find this version easier to explain: compare with low's top to choose a side, then fix the sizes. Stream 2, 9, 4, 6, 1.

Why it works

"low holds the smaller half and high the bigger half, with sizes equal or low one bigger. So in sorted order the median is exactly low's maximum, or the average of low's maximum and high's minimum — the two heap tops. Each add is O(log n), each median read O(1)."

The template to remember

PriorityQueue<Integer> low  = new PriorityQueue<>(Collections.reverseOrder());
PriorityQueue<Integer> high = new PriorityQueue<>();
void add(int x) {
    low.offer(x);
    high.offer(low.poll());                         // keep order between halves
    if (high.size() > low.size()) low.offer(high.poll()); // keep sizes
}
double median() {
    return low.size() > high.size() ? low.peek() : (low.peek() + high.peek()) / 2.0;
}
Remember

Small half in a max-heap, big half in a min-heap; the median lives at the doors. Add to low, push low's top to high, give one back if high got bigger.

Common mistakes
  • Using two min-heaps — low must be a max-heap so its top is the biggest of the small half.
  • Integer division: (7 + 8) / 2 is 7, not 7.5. Divide by 2.0.
  • (a, b) -> b - a overflows for values near ±2³¹; use Collections.reverseOrder().
  • Only balancing sizes and forgetting the order promise — a big number can end up in low.
  • Sliding window median needs removal of old numbers: use lazy deletion with a counter map, or two TreeMaps.

Test yourself

After 1, 2, 3, 4 have arrived, what is in low and high, and what is the median?

low = {1, 2} (top 2), high = {3, 4} (top 3). Median = (2 + 3) / 2 = 2.5.

Why is low a max-heap and high a min-heap, not the other way round?

The median is next to the boundary between the halves: the biggest small number and the smallest big number. Those are exactly the tops of a max-heap of the small half and a min-heap of the big half.

What is the cost of addNum and findMedian?

addNum O(log n) — at most three heap operations. findMedian O(1) — two peeks.
