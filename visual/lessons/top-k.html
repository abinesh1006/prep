Top K Elements

To keep the k biggest things, hold them in a min-heap of size k: the weakest winner sits on top, ready to be kicked out by anyone better.

Time to learn: about 1–2 hours · Prerequisites: Heaps, Arrays and hashing

When to use it — the signal

"k largest", "kth largest", "k smallest", "k most frequent", "k closest points", "top k words". A number k much smaller than n is the giveaway.

The idea in plain words

You want the 3 largest of [3, 2, 1, 5, 6, 4, 8]. Sorting everything costs O(n log n) and does far more work than needed.

Instead keep a small "winners" group of at most k = 3 numbers, stored in a min-heap. The top of a min-heap is its smallest member — the weakest winner.

  • Each new number joins the group.
  • If the group now has 4 members, kick out the weakest (poll the min).
  • At the end, the group holds the 3 largest, and its top is the 3rd largest.

The heap never grows past k, so each step costs O(log k). Total O(n log k) — with n = 1,000,000 and k = 10 that is far cheaper than sorting.

It feels backwards: largest → min-heap, smallest → max-heap. The heap's top must be the one you would throw out.

For most frequent, first count with a hash map, then pick the top k counts — either with the same size-k heap, or with buckets: an array where bucket[f] lists the numbers seen f times. Walking buckets from the highest f down gives O(n).

Analogy

A talent show with 3 chairs for finalists. When a new act beats the weakest finalist, the weakest one leaves their chair. You only ever need to know who the weakest finalist is — that is the top of the min-heap.

Why it works

"A min-heap of size k always holds the k largest seen so far, and its root is the smallest of them. Any new number that is not bigger than the root cannot be in the top k, so polling it loses nothing. Each step is O(log k), so the whole pass is O(n log k)."

The template to remember

// k LARGEST -> min-heap of size k (flip the comparator for k smallest)
PriorityQueue<T> heap = new PriorityQueue<>(byScoreAscending);
for (T x : items) {
    heap.offer(x);
    if (heap.size() > k) heap.poll();   // drop the weakest
}
// heap now holds the k best; heap.peek() is the kth best
Remember

Top k largest → min-heap of size k. Top k smallest → max-heap of size k. The top is the one you would throw out. Frequencies → count map first, then heap or buckets.

Common mistakes
  • Using a max-heap of all n items and polling k times: O(n + k log n) is fine, but O(n) memory — the size-k heap uses O(k).
  • Checking size() > k before offering: then the newcomer never gets compared.
  • Top K Frequent: putting raw numbers in the heap instead of comparing by count ((a, b) -> cnt.get(a) - cnt.get(b)).
  • Bucket array of size n instead of n + 1 — a number can appear n times.
  • Expecting the heap to come out sorted when you iterate it. Poll it if you need order.

Test yourself

You need the 5 smallest distances. Min-heap or max-heap, and of what size?

A max-heap of size 5. Its top is the largest of the 5 smallest — the one to throw out when a closer point arrives.

What is the time complexity of the size-k heap approach, and why is it better than sorting when k is small?

O(n log k). log k is tiny when k is small (log 10 ≈ 3), while sorting is O(n log n).

Why can the bucket approach for Top K Frequent be O(n)?

Frequencies are between 1 and n, so they can be array indices. Reading buckets from n down to 1 replaces sorting.
